Build proposal · Rev 2 · 26 Aug 2026

The Copper
Layer

The system design and integration front end for AV and IT, running on NCE. Take EasySchematic's good ideas, build them L1-first, store nothing of your own — and make the schema, the interchange formats and the verification loop speak the industry's standards rather than a private dialect.

Working name: Copper Store: NCE Module 6 Schema: NetBox-compatible

01

The inversion

One store, and it is the one you already own

EasySchematic is signal-flow-first. Its centre of gravity is the drawing: a .json file holding devices, ports, edges, canvas positions and rooms. The cable schedule, the pack list, the rack elevation are all derived paperwork, and an inventory system is at best an export target. That polarity is the whole problem — export means two stores both claiming to describe one building, reconciled by a human remembering to re-export.

L1-first fixes the polarity. But the useful move is not to make NetBox the store; it is to notice that you already have one. NCE Module 6 owns DEVICE, PORT, SIGNAL_CHAIN, RACK and CABLE as first-class node types, with tenancy, provenance, event replay and an MCP tool registry already around them.

Copper has no database. NCE is the store, the drawing is a projection of it, and NetBox is a schema to agree with — not a system to depend on.

02

Why agree with NetBox at all

Two payoffs, one condition

If NCE is the store, NetBox stops being infrastructure and becomes a convention. That is a much cheaper relationship — no Postgres-plus-Redis-plus-Django to carry, no plugin API to track, and nothing to install before a customer can use Copper. So why agree with its schema at all? Two reasons, and both are worth real money.

Payoff one · half the device library problem disappears

netbox-community/devicetype-library holds device type definitions for 314 manufacturers in YAML, licensed CC0-1.0 — public domain, no restrictions, commercial use fine. Each one defines exactly what Copper needs: interfaces, front ports, rear ports, console ports, power ports and outlets, module bays, device bays, u_height, is_full_depth, weight, airflow. Consuming it requires only that your port and component model matches NetBox's, which is the whole argument for schema compatibility.

But I read the manufacturer list, and it splits along exactly the AV / IT line. Strong on the IT side: Cisco, Juniper, Netgear, Ubiquiti, Ruckus, Fortinet, APC, Eaton, Middle Atlantic — the switch, firewall, UPS, power and rack layer, which is most of the IT half of an AV-over-IP job.

Absent: QSC, Extron, Crestron, Biamp, Shure, Sennheiser, Barco, Christie, Lightware, Kramer, Atlona, Symetrix, Powersoft, Genelec, Bose, ETC, Vaddio, Poly, Sony, Panasonic, AJA, Ross, Clear-Com, Audinate. Yamaha, Blackmagic Design and Canon are the only meaningful AV presences in 314.

So this is not the free replacement for EasySchematic's 3,884 AV templates. It is the free replacement for the IT catalogue, and the AV catalogue stays a real build item.

Which points at the leverage play

Author Bravo's AV device types in devicetype-library's own format and contribute them upstream under CC0. The format is already proven, the tooling to consume it already exists, and you get community maintenance on the long tail instead of owning four thousand definitions forever.

The ecosystem gets the AV coverage it visibly lacks; Bravo becomes the AV contributor in the NetBox world, which is worth more than a private catalogue and costs less to maintain. The alternative is a proprietary library — which is precisely the cost this architecture was meant to delete, paid in full.

One thing not to do: lift EasySchematic's device definitions. They are part of an AGPL-3.0 work, and the community submissions behind its hosted API have no clear grant at all. Its catalogue is a map of what to cover, not a source to copy.

Payoff two is the exit door. A customer who already runs NetBox can import their estate in one shot; a customer who leaves can export theirs. That removes the objection that kills infrastructure tools in procurement — nobody wants to hand their source of truth to a vendor with no way out. Being schema-compatible answers that without giving up ownership of the store.

What you should not do is emulate NetBox's REST API so that pynetbox and the Ansible collections work against Copper. Impersonating another product's API faithfully enough for its ecosystem, across its version history, is an unbounded chase for a payoff you will probably never collect. Agree with the schema; publish your own API.

Revision to Rev 1

Rev 1 said Copper needs about a fifth of NetBox's model. That was scoped to AV signal flow. Extending to IT brings more of it back — VLANs, prefixes and IP addressing are not optional once you are designing Dante and ST 2110 networks rather than drawing them. Still not circuits or wireless links, but budget for the IPAM core.

03

Standards, three ways

The part that makes it an integration tool

“Industry standards compatible” is not one requirement. It is three, with different costs, different payoffs, and different owners inside the programme — and conflating them is how a standards story turns into a logo wall.

The spine: one identifier, everywhere

Underneath all three sits the thing that actually makes integration work, and it is the least glamorous item on the page. A device on a drawing, a printed label on a cable, a line on a BOM, an asset in D365, a functional location in the FL hierarchy and an interface in a network capture all have to refer to the same physical object. Without a canonical reference designation, none of the compatibility above buys anything, because you cannot join the records.

That is a solved problem in adjacent industries — TIA-606 for telecom infrastructure administration and labelling, AVIXA's cable labelling standard for AV, IEC 81346 for reference designation in general. Adopt one, derive labels from it rather than typing them, and make it the primary key that crosses every boundary. The vendor-neutral naming rule from the D365 work is the same instinct; this generalises it.

04

What Copper inherits free

Because it runs on NCE

Running on NCE deletes most of what I costed in Rev 1 as “build your own store.” The comparison worth making is not Copper against EasySchematic; it is Copper against the standalone DCIM you would otherwise have to write underneath it.

Free

The store and the node types

Module 6 already owns DEVICE, PORT, SIGNAL_CHAIN, RACK and CABLE, with an AVIXA-parameter capabilities table underneath and propose-only validation behind validate_design_graph.

Free

Tenancy and access control

Namespaces and row-level security, already built and already load-bearing. Note the standing rule: scope every fetch explicitly by namespace rather than trusting RLS, because the owner pool bypasses it.

Free

The audit trail

Event types and replay. Design changes become events, so the change log that a DCIM has to build from scratch is a consequence of the architecture rather than a feature.

Free

The API, and the agent surface with it

The MCP tool registry with its cacheable, mutation and admin classification. Rev 1 argued for making the agent surface primary rather than a bolted-on beta; on NCE that is not a decision, it is the default. The canvas becomes one client among several.

Free

Provenance and reconciliation

Citations on answers, and the resolve and registry machinery for matching an external identifier to a node. Copper needs exactly this to reconcile a captured device against a drawn one.

Build

Cable path tracing

Walking a signal through front and rear port chains and passthrough hops, answering where it ends up and whether the path is complete. The one genuinely hard algorithm, the most important query the tool makes, and not something NCE has today.

Build

The physical validation body

A 2U device does not fit a 1U gap; a cable does not terminate on an occupied port. Individually trivial, collectively large, and the whole difference between a DCIM and a drawing toy.

Build

The canvas and the routing engine

Including trunk nesting, which is the long pole and stays the long pole under every option on the table.

05

The loop this closes

The reason to build rather than buy

Of the five open-ended quote origination paths, one is literally design-generated — and it currently has no producer. A drawn design emitting BOM_LINEs with per-line provenance is not a feature request, it is a path with nothing feeding it. Because provenance is per line rather than per quote, a mixed quote of drawn devices plus manually picked accessories plus a package expansion stays correctly attributed.

At the other end, the BOM_LINE status machine runs ORDERED to DELIVERED to INSTALLED to TESTED. Per my notes only DELIVERED ever got a writer wave. INSTALLED and TESTED are exactly the moment a drawing becomes an as-built — and with a verification standard behind it, TESTED stops being a checkbox and becomes evidence.

Two smaller hooks worth naming. Engine 19 puts state on the drawing — RMM health against the same device nodes means a signal chain that greys out at the fault, which is a support tool rather than a picture. And the D365 functional location work gets simpler: its v2 schema concluded that columns cannot carry topology because ports and edges need their own row types. Going node-native dissolves that. You stop shipping a ten-sheet workbook and import to objects, with the FL hierarchy landing as site and location.

06

The four hard problems

Where this actually gets difficult

1 · Which Module 6 tools actually write

This is the first thing to check, and it may be the whole first wave. My notes say Module 6's last wave added no tools because its work was flow-invoked, and that its five validation queries are propose-only. If that is still true, Module 6 has node types, a schema and validation but no write surface for DEVICE, PORT or CABLE — and Copper cannot persist a single dragged device until someone writes those tools.

The programme's own rule applies here with force: never design against a seam that is planned rather than wired. Verify the tool surface before anything else, because the answer changes the first month of work entirely.

2 · Design intent versus record

NCE records what is. AV design is about what will be — half-finished, speculative, three competing options for the same rack. Copper needs a draft concept, and now it needs it inside NCE rather than in a document. Options are a status on the node, a separate draft namespace promoted on commit, or draft-scoped nodes; the honest answer is that whoever owns the merge semantics owns the hard part, and it should be decided before the first write lands rather than after.

Worth noting that this is the one thing EasySchematic gets free by being a document, and the one place the document model is genuinely better.

3 · Signal type has no home in the NetBox schema

EasySchematic carries 73 signal types and colour-codes every edge by them. NetBox's vocabulary is Ethernet-centric — rich for networking media, thin for everything else — and a large share of AV is not an interface at all: analog audio, DMX, speaker line, contact closure, intercom. Front and rear ports cover some of it generically.

So signal type is an extension you own, sitting alongside the compatible schema rather than inside it, plus the compatibility matrix that drives adapter insertion. Unavoidable, and also the single most valuable thing Copper would own, because nobody else models AV signal semantics on top of a source of truth.

4 · The schedule, not the software

NCE has a long wave queue and a documented habit of waves that ratify their own shortfalls. Copper is a large new surface on a system that is still mid-build, and the realistic risk is not that it is technically wrong but that it sits behind ninety other things and lands half-wired. Sizing it as a side project only works if it is scoped as a genuinely thin front end over seams that already exist — which is exactly what problem one determines.

Licence hygiene

EasySchematic is AGPL-3.0. Architecture and approach are not copyrightable; its routing implementation is. Read it to understand trunk nesting, then write your own, and do not paste — AGPL section 13 would oblige you to offer source to every user reaching a Bravo-facing service over a network.

devicetype-library is CC0-1.0, so the catalogue is genuinely free to use. NetBox core is Apache 2.0. And netbox-branching is PolyForm Shield 1.0.0 — not open source, and it forbids competing with the licensor. Not a dependency under this architecture, which is one more reason the architecture is better.

07

Where to start, and what would make me wrong

Ordered by what unblocks what

  • Audit the Module 6 write surface. Not a build step — a two-hour read that determines everything after it. Which of the five node types have mutation tools, and is validate_design_graph still propose-only?
  • Import devicetype-library. The catalogue before the canvas — it proves the schema decision cheaply and is useful the moment it lands. Expect it to cover the IT layer and almost none of the AV layer.
  • Then author the AV catalogue in the same format, starting with the brands Bravo actually specifies, and contribute it upstream. This is the largest unglamorous work item on the list and the easiest one to under-plan.
  • Read-only projection of one site. Laid out automatically from real nodes, with a cable schedule out. No writes, no drafts. Immediately useful on the Veidekke core stack, and the cheapest possible way to discover the premise is wrong.
  • Then the signal-type extension, because colour coding, validation, adapters and AV-meaningful paperwork all depend on it and nothing depends on them.
  • Then drafts and the first writes, blocked on the decision in 06.2 — so make that decision before you need it.
  • Then BOM_LINE emission, which is what turns a drawing tool into part of the business.
  • Routing quality is a continuous track, not a milestone. Naive orthogonal routing ships; trunk nesting is where drawings start looking professional. Budget it as ongoing.

What would make this wrong: the L1-first premise rests on the gear being modellable as nodes and ports. If a large share of what Bravo actually draws is non-rack, non-networked, one-off kit — loose speakers, trim plates, furniture-mounted plates, runs with no endpoint device — then you spend your time fighting the object model instead of benefiting from it, and a document-first tool with an exporter starts looking reasonable again. Sampling a few real projects settles that cheaply, and so does the read-only projection step.

Two smaller unknowns: whether the front and rear port model survives contact with real AV patch fields at scale, and how much routing quality is reachable before it stops being worth the effort. Neither is answerable from here.
